\section{Purpose of This Template}

A dissertation is both a sustained research argument and a structured document. LaTeX supports that combination by separating content from formatting and by automating numbering, cross-references, citations, and lists~\cite{lamport1994latex,mittelbach2023latex}. This chapter demonstrates how to introduce a topic, define the problem, state research questions, summarize contributions, and preview the remaining chapters. The subject matter is deliberately generic so that writers can replace it with material from any discipline.

The complete dissertation is compiled from \texttt{thesis\_main.tex}. Individual chapters remain in their own folders and are inserted with \verb|\input{folder/main}|. This arrangement keeps a long project readable and makes collaboration easier. For example, Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review} demonstrates thematic synthesis, and Chapter~\ref{ch:paper3} demonstrates reporting experimental results.

\section{Motivation and Problem Statement}

Long academic documents contain many interacting parts: front matter, chapters, figures, tables, equations, appendices, citations, and institution-specific formatting. Managing all of these elements in one source file can make revision difficult. A modular project reduces that burden by assigning each chapter and supporting asset a predictable location.

The general problem addressed by this template is therefore:

\begin{quote}
How can a dissertation project be organized so that its content remains modular while formatting, numbering, and cross-references remain consistent across the complete document?
\end{quote}

\section{Example Research Questions}

Research questions should be specific enough to guide analysis and broad enough to support a meaningful contribution~\cite{booth2016craft}. A template dissertation might use questions such as:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How does the proposed approach compare with an established baseline?
    \item Which design factors most strongly influence the measured outcome?
    \item Under what conditions do the findings remain valid?
\end{enumerate}

Replace these examples with questions that match the problem statement, methods, and evidence in the actual dissertation.

\section{Example Contributions}

A contribution statement tells readers what the dissertation adds. Depending on the discipline, contributions may include a theory, framework, dataset, method, artifact, empirical finding, or design recommendation. A concise list can make the scope visible:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a clearly defined research problem and conceptual framework;
    \item a reproducible method for collecting and analyzing evidence;
    \item an evaluation that compares alternatives using appropriate measures; and
    \item an interpretation of findings, limitations, and implications.
\end{itemize}
